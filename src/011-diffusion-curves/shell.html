diffusion curves

Curves with a colour on each side. Everything between them is the actual solution to Laplace's equation — not a blur, not a mesh of control points. Solved in your browser, one independent random walk per pixel.

To evaluate this you need to understand five things.
  1. The claim is mathematical, not aesthetic. The field between the curves satisfies Δu = 0 with the curve colours as boundary data. A Gaussian blur of the same curves looks similar and is a different function.
  2. The test for that claim is the mean-value property. A harmonic function's value at any point equals its average over any circle around that point that stays clear of the boundary. The build's oracle checks exactly this, on the rendered pixels, in a real browser.
  3. It is Monte Carlo, so it is noisy and the noise is the honest part. Error falls as 1/√samples. Doubling samples does not double quality; quadrupling halves the error.
  4. Your artwork never leaves your machine. The SVG is parsed by your own browser and the pixels are never uploaded — there is no account, no storage, and no image ever reaches a server. Being exact about the one exception: the page sends a single cookieless first-party ping recording that this URL was visited, and nothing else. No file, no pixels, no identifier.
  5. Software rendering is slow. On a machine without a working GPU path this falls back to a software rasteriser and a high sample count takes minutes. That is the honest cost of a per-pixel stochastic solver.

What it does that a mesh gradient does not

Mesh-gradient tools give you a grid of control points and interpolate between them. Diffusion curves invert that: you specify colour on curves, and the smooth field between them is computed rather than authored. Sharp edges stay sharp — the two sides of a curve are independent boundary values, so a hard colour break survives arbitrarily close to the line, which is exactly what a control-point mesh cannot do.

How the solve works

Grid-free walk-on-spheres, after Sawhney's grid-free Monte Carlo methods for PDEs. For each pixel: find the distance to the nearest curve, jump to a uniformly random point on the circle of that radius, repeat. When the walk lands on a curve, take the colour of the side it arrived from. Average many such walks. There is no mesh, no linear system, and every pixel is independent — which is why it is a fragment shader and not a solver.

The image border is a boundary too, tinted with the page background, otherwise the problem is not well posed on a finite canvas.

Limits, stated

  • Solve resolution is selectable from 128² to 512² and the canvas is displayed scaled with nearest-neighbour. Export is the solved image at the chosen resolution. Cost is linear in pixels × samples, so 512² is 16× the work of 128².
  • Dirichlet data on segments only — no Neumann conditions, no source terms, no blur radius per curve. Curved paths are sampled into segments.
  • Up to 12 curves and 64 segments from an SVG.
  • Colour is averaged in sRGB, not linear light. Physically that is the wrong space; it is the space the boundary colours are specified in, and changing it would change what "the colour you set" means at the boundary.
